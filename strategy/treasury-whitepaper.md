# Treasury White Paper: The National Renewal Fund

## 1. Executive mandate and purpose

The National Renewal Fund (NRF) is a permanent, publicly owned investment vehicle designed to build and retain a national portfolio of productive energy infrastructure.

Its objectives are to strengthen energy security, reduce long-term energy-system costs, improve productivity and create an income-generating public asset for future generations.

The NRF uses a capital-to-asset model. Capital contributions are ring-fenced for productive infrastructure and operating revenues are reinvested until the Fund can finance its approved long-term investment programme from recurring cashflows.

The NRF is therefore **designed for long-term fiscal self-sufficiency**. Its final UK public-sector accounting and fiscal classification must be established with the appropriate statistical, Treasury and legal authorities.

## 2. Capitalisation framework

### Individuals

A decadal contribution applies to individuals with more than £100 million in UK-based net assets, subject to detailed statutory valuation, residence and liquidity rules.

### Corporate groups and UK Economic Footprint

Corporate liability is assessed on a consolidated group's **UK Economic Footprint Revenue (UK-EFR)** rather than simply the turnover reported by a UK subsidiary.

UK-EFR includes third-party sales to UK customers and UK-user-linked advertising, marketplace, intermediation and digital-service revenues, regardless of the contracting entity or invoice location. Connected entities are aggregated and anti-fragmentation rules apply.

A £25 million group allowance applies before the contribution is calculated.

### Contribution tiers

- **1% Voluntary Tier** — proactive participation and Founding Partner eligibility.
- **2% Statutory Tier** — standard statutory contribution.
- **3% Enforcement Tier** — applicable where enforcement action is required.

### Affordability ceiling

Corporate liability is subject to a ceiling of 20% of **Adjusted UK Economic Profit (AUEP)** for the relevant decadal cycle.

AUEP is defined using the higher of:

1. UK tax-adjusted operating profit after appropriate arm's-length corrections; or
2. the group's consolidated pre-tax profit apportioned to the UK through a statutory economic-footprint formula weighted 70% to UK customer/user revenue, 15% to UK payroll/headcount and 15% to UK tangible assets.

The purpose is to protect genuinely low-margin economic activity without allowing royalties, interest, management charges, profit shifting, subsidiary location or artificial cashflow arrangements to reduce the ceiling improperly.

Genuine liquidity difficulty may justify HMRC-approved instalments. It does not reduce the liability: deferred sums remain due, may carry interest and may be secured. Genuine loss positions may be carried forward under the statutory rules rather than automatically extinguished.

The detailed design requires specialist tax-law review and company-level modelling before legislation.

## 3. Initial capitalisation potential

UK private-sector businesses employing 250 or more people generate approximately **£2.7 trillion in annual turnover**. A contribution equivalent to 1% of that turnover represents approximately **£27 billion** before application of the NRF eligibility rules, £25 million group allowance, UK-EFR allocation, affordability ceiling, sector coverage and behavioural effects.

The individual contribution represents a further potential source of capital. Independent research into UK households with wealth above £100 million has estimated that a 2% annual minimum wealth tax could raise approximately £10 billion per year. The NRF is a different, limited decadal mechanism, so this evidence establishes potential scale rather than an NRF revenue forecast.

No combined launch-revenue figure should be treated as a scored forecast until the final design has been modelled against company- and household-level data. Low, central and high scenarios should be independently produced before implementation.

Capital deployment is therefore staged against verified receipts rather than committed against an assumed headline revenue figure.

## 4. Investment strategy

### Phase 1 — Storage first

Grid-scale storage is the first system priority. No technology receives a guaranteed preferred position.

Sodium-ion is one candidate alongside LFP and other battery chemistries, pumped hydro, flow batteries, compressed-air storage, thermal storage and future technologies.

Competitive tenders should assess:

- whole-life cost per MWh delivered;
- discharge duration;
- lifetime cycles and degradation;
- round-trip efficiency;
- safety;
- recyclability;
- UK supply-chain content and resilience;
- build time;
- land and grid constraints;
- availability; and
- expected grid-service and arbitrage revenues.

### Phase 2 — Renewable generation

Expand commercially appropriate wind, solar, tidal and other generation, integrated with the storage fleet.

### Phase 3 — Electrification and efficiency

Support network resilience, productive electrification and efficiency measures capable of reducing long-term system costs.

### Phase 4 — Interconnection and exports

Develop international interconnection and export opportunities where they strengthen domestic resilience and create attractive risk-adjusted returns.

## 5. Governance and independence

The NRF is an independent statutory body operating commercially at arm's length from day-to-day government.

All capital and operating revenues are legally ring-fenced for the Fund's statutory energy-infrastructure mandate.

Its governance architecture is intended to **create exceptional statutory and institutional barriers to political interference**. Material changes to the founding purpose or Sunset Clause require enhanced parliamentary approval together with formal approval through the independent governance structure.

Accounts are independently audited. A public National Energy Dashboard reports asset deployment, storage and generation capacity, operating performance and revenue reinvestment.

## 6. Sunset Clause

The contribution mechanism is permanently rescinded when independently audited forecasts demonstrate that recurring NRF operating cashflows, after maintenance, required reserves and statutory resilience requirements, are sufficient to finance the Fund's approved long-term infrastructure investment programme without further external capital contributions.

A maximum of four decadal contribution cycles is an absolute backstop. Operational self-sufficiency may terminate contributions earlier.

## 7. Why not fund the same infrastructure through government borrowing?

Sovereign borrowing is a legitimate comparator and must form part of the final appraisal.

The NRF pursues a different capital-formation model: finite contributions from extreme private wealth and the largest UK economic footprints are converted directly into ring-fenced productive assets. The model creates a visible social compact and seeks to establish a permanent national balance-sheet asset without relying upon an open-ended addition to conventional borrowing.

A full appraisal should compare the NRF with gilts-funded investment, public corporations, Contracts for Difference, regulated-asset-base finance and private investment rather than assuming the NRF is inherently superior.

## 8. Relationship to the National Wealth Fund

The NRF is intended to complement rather than duplicate the UK's existing National Wealth Fund.

The National Wealth Fund deploys public financial capacity across multiple strategic sectors and seeks to mobilise wider private investment. The NRF has a narrower purpose: it is a dedicated national energy endowment designed to build and retain a permanent, income-generating public energy asset base, with a finite external capitalisation mechanism and a defined route to operational self-sufficiency.

The detailed White Paper should assess opportunities for cooperation and ensure that the two institutions do not duplicate investment unnecessarily.
